Author

Mariana Vejar

Introduction

In the economics of charitable giving, a central question is whether the “price” of giving affects donor behavior. Dean Karlan and John List designed a natural field experiment to explore this by mailing over 50,000 donation solicitations to previous contributors of a politically liberal nonprofit. The solicitations randomly varied the presence and size of a matching grant — where a “leadership donor” promised to match contributions at ratios of $1:$1, $2:$1, or $3:$1 — to see how this influenced the likelihood and size of donations.

This intervention simulates a price change: for example, under a $2:$1 match, a $1 donation turns into $3 for the organization, lowering the “price” of public good provision from $1 to 33 cents. But does this lead people to give more?

This blog post replicates some of Karlan and List’s main findings using their publicly shared data. We aim to explore how the match offer affected donation behavior, whether larger match ratios increased giving, and what this implies about human motivation in philanthropic settings.

Description

The dataset used in this analysis was provided by Karlan and List as part of their 2007 publication. It contains 50,083 observations — one for each individual who received a fundraising letter during the experiment.

Each observation includes variables related to: - Treatment assignment: whether the individual received a matching offer and what kind. - Donor behavior: including whether they gave (gave) and how much (amount). - Experimental conditions: such as the matching ratio offered (ratio), the suggested donation (ask), and the match threshold (size). - Demographics and history: such as gender (female), number of prior donations (freq), and previous contribution size (hpa).

This dataset enables a detailed analysis of how price framing and appeal design influence charitable behavior in a real-world setting.

Balance Test

I assess whether randomization produced statistically similar groups by testing balance on pre-treatment variables. Here we focus on mrm2, which captures the number of months since the last donation.

Our t-test and regression yield nearly identical results, as expected. We fail to reject the null hypothesis that treatment and control differ on mrm2. This supports the validity of the randomization. Table 1 in the original paper presents similar summary statistics, highlighting that observable covariates were balanced across experimental conditions.

Experimental Results

I conducted a balance check on mrm2 (months since last donation), a pre-treatment variable. Both a t-test and a bivariate regression confirm there is no statistically significant difference between treatment and control groups (p ≈ 0.905). This indicates that randomization was successful.

The coefficient from the regression is 0.014 (p = 0.905), meaning treatment and control groups differed by less than 1/50th of a month — an economically and statistically negligible amount.

Table 1 in Karlan & List (2007) serves a similar purpose: it demonstrates that observed covariates are balanced across groups, supporting the credibility of causal claims. Including such a table — and running our own check — assures readers that any observed treatment effects are not artifacts of selection bias.

Next, I assess the effectiveness of different sizes of matched donations on the response rate.

The 2:1 and 3:1 match ratios lead to slightly higher donation rates than the 1:1 ratio (2.26% and 2.27% vs. 2.07%). However, these differences are not statistically significant. This supports the authors’ claim that larger match ratios do not meaningfully affect giving behavior, a finding that runs counter to traditional fundraising wisdom.

The regression suggests that match offers increase donation amount by about $0.15, but the result is only marginally statistically significant (p ≈ 0.06). This implies that matching mostly encourages more people to donate, rather than making donors give much more money.

💡 What We Learn Even if a match doesn’t strongly affect how much people give on average, it still nudges more people to give (as shown earlier). So the main power of a match lies in boosting participation, rather than gift size. This pattern also appears in Karlan and List (2007), who emphasized the increase in participation rather than donation amount per se.

Conditional on Giving: Do Donors Give More?

Next, I limit our sample to individuals who gave and repeat our analysis.

The treatment group gave about $1.67 less than the control group, but this difference is not statistically significant (p ≈ 0.56). This suggests that matching offers do not affect how much people give, conditional on donating.

However, this regression does not have a causal interpretation, since we’re conditioning on a post-treatment variable (gave). Instead, it just describes the behavior of the subgroup of people who donated.

💡 What We Learn The match offer increased participation, but not the donation amount among those who were already going to give. This aligns with Karlan and List’s interpretation that matching is effective at expanding the pool of donors, rather than increasing generosity among existing ones.

Simulation Experiment

As a reminder of how the t-statistic “works,” in this section I use simulation to demonstrate the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem.

Suppose the true distribution of respondents who do not get a charitable donation match is Bernoulli with probability p=0.018 that a donation is made.

Further suppose that the true distribution of respondents who do get a charitable donation match of any size is Bernoulli with probability p=0.022 that a donation is made.

Law of Large Numbers

To demonstrate how the sample average converges to the true treatment effect, I simulate donation behavior under the assumed probabilities (p=0.018 for control, p=0.022 for treatment):

This chart illustrates the Law of Large Numbers: although the early estimates are noisy, the cumulative average of simulated differences gradually stabilizes around the true effect of 0.004 as the sample size grows.

Central Limit Theorem

We simulate the average treatment effect at sample sizes of 50, 200, 500, and 1000. For each size, we run 1,000 simulations, where we take n random draws from both control and treatment distributions and compute the difference in means.

As the sample size increases, the histogram of simulated differences becomes narrower and more bell-shaped. This is a visual demonstration of the Central Limit Theorem, which shows why large sample sizes are critical for accurate statistical inference.
